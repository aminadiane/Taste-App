What the Bronx Can Teach Us About the Future of AI

Why AI capital should flow through the people who already hold a community’s trust.

AMINA DIANÉ·OCT 2026·7 MIN READ

Picture Suzy, a salon owner in the South Bronx. Suzy is not unlike many of the women I worked with and employed through my economic mobility startup, Meilleur. She understands her neighborhood and the women who come to her chair in ways that extend far beyond their preferences in how they like to style their hair and nails. Suzy’s speech patterns, which match those of her community, as well as her style, skills, and cultural knowledge make her an insider and give her a level of status and reverence in her community that would take years or even decades for a perceived outsider to earn.

I saw this influence firsthand growing up in New York City, where I had a front-row seat to the extremes of human flourishing and human suffering, often within the same few blocks. For as long as I can remember, the biggest difference between the people who got to live dignified lives and the people who did not has come down to capital.

Capital comes in more than one form, and each can help a person climb the economic ladder. My attention is focused on three in particular: economic capital, the form everyone knows; cultural capital, the version coined by French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu; and AI capital, the newest of the three, which is being doled out right now.

Economist Nick Drydakis defines AI capital as AI-related knowledge and capabilities that improve a person's employment prospects and economic outcomes. I posit, however, that AI capital matters far beyond its capacity to optimize employment. Instead, its greatest value lies in the alternative realities it empowers people to build today – realities that may exist entirely outside the confines of our current economy, job market, and social structures as we understand them today. That is, AI is not merely preparing people for the future of work; it is enabling them, at this very moment, to engineer entirely new systems and economic horizons from the ground up.

In communities like Suzy’s, cultural capital – and the social capital it often engenders – determines who is trusted, and trust determines what gets adopted. If AI capital is going to reach these communities without reproducing inequality, it must flow through the people who hold that trust.

To tell the story of the future, we first need to go back about 50 years in the past. In the 1970s, while much of the South Bronx burned, a teenager named Clive Campbell, who would become known as DJ Kool Herc, extended the instrumental breaks of funk records so the crowd could keep dancing. This moment marked the birth of hip hop, created with practically no economic capital, in what was and continues to be the poorest congressional district in the country. The Bronx gifted the world a global genre, a new vocabulary, and a distinct aesthetic, yet the vast wealth those cultural exports generated was overwhelmingly captured by corporate record labels and outside brands. Unless we intentionally design a different infrastructure today, AI capital is poised to replicate this exact extraction pattern.

AI is not merely preparing people for the future of work; it is enabling them, at this very moment, to engineer entirely new systems and economic horizons from the ground up.

To this effect, when AI adoption spreads through a community’s cultural networks, the value it generates is fundamentally built on local trust. Ensuring this value is retained requires mechanisms that allow the gains of adoption to systematically flow back into the community, rather than concentrating entirely within external technology platforms. Without these structural loops, initiatives risk creating an extractive model by diverting the wealth generated by a community away from the ecosystem that fostered it. To break this cycle, any program worth funding must ensure that what Suzy builds remains hers: her clients, her data, and her revenue.

Consider what this could look like for Suzy. Today, a salon owner like her could use AI to handle the bookings, bookkeeping, and marketing that once required staff she could not afford. For example, she could turn her decades of expertise into a training academy for the next generation of stylists in her neighborhood, with an AI tutor that teaches technique in her voice and supplementarily prepares students for the state licensing exam at a fraction of what cosmetology school costs. She could link her shop with other salons across the borough to form a cooperative that shares one booking system, buys supplies in bulk, and pools part of its revenue into a lending circle for members who want to open their own chairs. Further, the knowledge she has built over a lifetime could become an asset in the form of valuable IP that she licenses on her own terms, which could include her expertise in textured hair, which products actually work, and what her community wants before anyone else knows it. But, this only represents a small sliver of what is possible when AI capital is distributed in economically vulnerable populations. Scaling her impact through AI means Suzy can reshape her community at a completely new magnitude.

Cultural capital determines who is trusted, and trust determines what gets adopted.

Consider the following: Because Suzy’s clients already trust her with more than their hair, her salon could become the neighborhood’s front door to AI and the world of opportunities that it makes possible. It could be the place where a mother gets help filing for benefits, a teenager drafts a first résumé, or a new business owner navigates a city permit, with AI handling the paperwork while Suzy provides the trust and entry point to AI capital that makes it possible.

The people closest to a problem are usually best positioned to solve it.

In a neighborhood where economic capital is scarce, cultural capital and the social prestige it brings can dictate who children look up to and who parents respect. It determines who is trusted to launch neighborhood initiatives, who is given the mandate to open and run schools, and who must provide a cosign before an outside organization can effectively set up shop.

The reason to start with leaders like Suzy, rather than with her clients directly, is the same reason perceived outsiders would likely struggle to achieve the same success were they to set up shop on her street. If Suzy adopts a tool, her stylists will learn it, her clients will hear about it, and her neighbors will trust it. If she rejects it, it is very possible that no amount of outside funding will make it stick. An investment in Suzy is not only an investment in her business; it is an investment in one of the most reliable distribution channels her community has.

I learned this firsthand from the funding side. After graduating from UPenn, I had the opportunity to advise MacKenzie Scott’s multi-billion-dollar philanthropic initiative through my work with The Bridgespan Group. What struck me most was how much of that giving came without strings attached. Rather than dictating how funds should be spent, the initiative trusted community-led organizations to understand their own needs better than any outside funder could. It was an approach built on the same premise that I am proposing in this essay: the people closest to a problem are usually best positioned to solve it.

To prevent AI from widening the global inequality gap in neighborhoods like Suzy’s, philanthropy must provide community leaders with a dual infusion of capital.

First, they need economic capital in the form of grants substantial enough to buy back their time. With financial breathing room, Suzy could delegate her shop's daily operational tasks and dedicate those reclaimed hours to mastering AI tools. Second, she needs AI capital: direct access to the software alongside the targeted training required to integrate AI into her business and leverage it for her community.

A pilot could begin with a small cohort of community leaders like Suzy over a twelve-month period. Each leader would receive a grant large enough to buy back their time, allowing them to step away from the daily strain of keeping their businesses and households afloat and redirect those hours toward mastering AI. Along with financial backing, the program would provide direct access to advanced AI tools and ongoing, structured training to help them deploy those technologies effectively.

Success would be measured not only by revenue increases and economic opportunities, but by time reclaimed, new services created, and how many people in each leader’s network adopted these tools through them. If, after a year, the gains have flowed mostly to software vendors rather than extending value creation to the leaders and their communities, that would be the signal to redesign before scaling.

This approach has risks of its own. Funders need a way to find leaders like Suzy without solely relying on outside consultants, and a strategy built around trusted figures can miss people outside their networks. One potential route is to let communities nominate their own leaders and to fund several in each neighborhood rather than one.

AI capital is being distributed right now. If we want to deploy it effectively, we should start with community leaders like Suzy, whose local salon operates as a beacon of the neighborhood, or kids like Clive Campbell, who was able to create a global cultural movement in the 1970s with virtually zero financial resources. They anchored their neighborhoods and changed the world on the strength of their cultural capital alone. Imagine what they could build if given access to the most powerful machines in human history.
